## Part 2: Frequently Asked Viva Voce Questions & Answers

### Q1: Why is Multinomial Naive Bayes preferred over standard Gaussian Naive Bayes for text classification?
> **Answer:** Gaussian Naive Bayes assumes that continuous features follow a Gaussian (normal) distribution. However, text document representations (like TF-IDF or word counts) are discrete, non-negative, and highly sparse (over 95% of vocabulary values for any single document are zero). Multinomial Naive Bayes models feature counts generated by a multinomial distribution, which accurately mirrors word frequency patterns in natural language.

### Q2: Why is Laplace (Add-1) smoothing necessary in Naive Bayes?
> **Answer:** Under the Naive Bayes assumption, class likelihood is the product of individual word probabilities: $P(\mathbf{x} \mid c) = \prod_{i=1}^n P(w_i \mid c)$. If an incoming test review contains even a single word that never occurred in class $c$ during training, its empirical frequency $N_{ci} = 0$, causing $P(w_i \mid c) = 0$. Because of multiplication, the entire class probability collapses to zero, completely overriding all other strong sentiment words in the sentence. Laplace smoothing adds $\alpha=1$ to the numerator and $\alpha|V|$ to the denominator:
> $$P(w_i \mid c) = \frac{N_{ci} + 1}{N_c + |V|}$$
> This guarantees every word maintains a small non-zero probability.

### Q3: What is data leakage and how is it prevented in your code?
> **Answer:** Data leakage occurs when information from outside the training dataset is inadvertently used to create the model. In NLP, this typically happens when the `TfidfVectorizer` is fitted on the entire dataset before splitting. Doing so allows the test set's vocabulary and document frequency distribution (IDF) to leak into training.  
> In our project, we prevent leakage by using `sklearn.pipeline.Pipeline`:
> ```python
> Pipeline([('tfidf', build_tfidf_vectorizer()), ('classifier', MultinomialNB())])
> ```
> The vectorizer's vocabulary and IDF weights are computed **strictly** on `X_train`. When `predict()` or `transform()` is called on `X_test`, it uses only the pre-computed training parameters without updating them.

### Q4: Why is removing words like "not" or "never" as standard stopwords dangerous in sentiment analysis?
> **Answer:** In traditional topic modeling, removing stopwords like *"not"* or *"never"* reduces noise without altering topic semantics. However, in sentiment analysis, these words act as **polarity shifters (valence shifters)**. If a user writes *"The service was not good"*, a standard stopword filter deletes *"not"*, leaving only *"service good"*, which causes the model to classify a negative complaint as positive! Our preprocessing pipeline implements custom negation preservation (`NEGATION_WORDS`), keeping critical negation words intact.

### Q5: Does Multinomial Naive Bayes use Gradient Descent for optimization?
> **Answer:** No. Multinomial Naive Bayes has a closed-form analytic solution. Its parameters—the prior probabilities $P(c)$ and conditional feature probabilities $P(w_i \mid c)$—are calculated directly via counting and relative frequency division (Maximum Likelihood Estimation).  
> To demonstrate DSC Unit 7 (Optimization), our system trains a comparative **Logistic Regression** model, which cannot be solved in closed form and explicitly utilizes iterative gradient optimization (L-BFGS quasi-Newton method) to minimize cross-entropy loss.

### Q6: What is the difference between Macro F1-score and Weighted F1-score?
> **Answer:**
> - **Macro F1-score:** Calculates the unweighted arithmetic mean of F1-scores across all classes:
>   $$\text{F1}_{\text{macro}} = \frac{\text{F1}_{\text{pos}} + \text{F1}_{\text{neg}} + \text{F1}_{\text{neu}}}{3}$$
>   It treats all classes equally, making it sensitive to poor performance on minority classes.
> - **Weighted F1-score:** Weights the F1-score of each class by its support (true instance count in the dataset):
>   $$\text{F1}_{\text{weighted}} = \sum_{c} \frac{N_c}{N} \text{F1}_c$$
>   When class imbalance exists, Macro F1 is a more honest indicator of classifier quality across underrepresented categories.

### Q7: Explain how OLTP and OLAP are demonstrated in your database architecture.
> **Answer:**
> - **OLTP (Online Transaction Processing):** Demonstrated when a user analyzes a single review or uploads a CSV file. Individual record inserts (`INSERT INTO predictions ...`) execute with ACID guarantees and row-level indexing for high transaction throughput.
> - **OLAP (Online Analytical Processing):** Demonstrated on the **Prediction History** and **Analytics** dashboard pages, where SQL aggregation queries (`COUNT`, `GROUP BY predicted_sentiment`, date-range filters) compute distribution summaries and KPIs across stored historical predictions.

## Part 3: Step-by-Step Live Classroom Demonstration Script

**Total Duration:** 5 to 7 Minutes  
**Audience:** College Professor / External Viva Examiner

### Phase 1: Problem Introduction (1 Minute)
1. Open the browser to `http://localhost:8501`.
2. Introduce the project:
   > *"Good morning respected evaluators. Today I present our Data Science and Computing capstone project: Social Media Sentiment Analysis System. The objective is to analyze unstructured social comments and customer feedback into three sentiment classes: Positive, Negative, and Neutral using NLP, TF-IDF feature extraction, and Multinomial Naive Bayes classification."*
3. Highlight the KPI banner showing total analyzed reviews and model accuracy.

### Phase 2: Live Single Inference Demonstration (1.5 Minutes)
1. Click on **Sentiment Analyzer** from the sidebar navigation.
2. Select the quick example button **😊 Positive Review** $\rightarrow$ click **🔍 Analyze Sentiment**.
3. Point out:
   - The predicted badge (`Positive`).
   - The probability bar chart breakdown (e.g., Positive: 87.5%, Negative: 6.0%, Neutral: 6.5%).
   - The preprocessed text card showing tokenization and lowercasing.
4. Now type a tricky negation sentence:
   ```
   "The battery life is not good and I am never buying this again."
   ```
5. Click **🔍 Analyze Sentiment**. Show the examiner that the system correctly predicts **Negative** because negation words (`not`, `never`) were safely preserved by our custom preprocessing pipeline!
6. Click **💾 Save Prediction to Database**.

### Phase 3: Bulk CSV Analysis Demonstration (1.5 Minutes)
1. Navigate to **Bulk Analysis** in the sidebar.
2. Upload a sample CSV file or use the sample records.
3. Select the text column $\rightarrow$ Click **🚀 Run Bulk Analysis**.
4. Highlight:
   - Automated batch prediction across all rows.
   - Interactive Plotly donut and count charts showing sentiment distribution.
   - The filtered preview table.
   - The **📥 Download Processed CSV** button.

### Phase 4: Model Evaluation & DSC Syllabus Justification (1.5 Minutes)
1. Navigate to **Model Evaluation**.
2. Show the **Confusion Matrix Heatmap** and explain diagonal True Positives versus off-diagonal misclassifications.
3. Show the comparison table between **Multinomial Naive Bayes** and **Logistic Regression**, explaining the closed-form vs. gradient descent optimization distinction (Unit 7).
4. Navigate to **Analytics** to show the 7-day rolling moving average sentiment trends (Unit 9).
5. Open **Prediction History** to show the database table where our previous live test review was persisted (Unit 8).

### Phase 5: Conclusion & Q&A
> *"In conclusion, this project implements all 10 DSC syllabus units into a complete, modular, and deployable software application. Thank you, and I am happy to answer any questions."*
